WHERE THIS IS GOING

Surface area is the largest unused resource on earth.

Every piece of intelligence we have built still lives inside something manufactured: a data centre, a chip, a phone. A box built in a ten-billion-dollar plant, connected to a grid, and placed somewhere.

Everything else is inert. Every building, hull, panel, bridge and aircraft skin — the entire surface of the built world — does nothing at all.

Its computational value today is not low. It is zero.

The age of intelligence will need it in a trillion places, and nobody is going to wire a trillion boxes or charge a trillion batteries. The sensor has to vanish into the surface that was going on anyway.

Nobody has to install anything. The coating was always going on. No new component to approve, no process to change, no cable to run.

Computed live from a two-time-constant circuit model of a porous oxide on metal. Ageing is modelled as loss of effective contact area: resistance rises and capacitance falls together, so the time constants — the shape — are unchanged while the height is not. The shape marker is the half-fall point of each sweep after that sweep is normalised against itself, which is why it survives any uniform gain or area factor. The shapes and directions are representative of published behaviour; the numbers are not measurements of our material, and nothing in the three open questions has been measured on our own film.

Push the ageing slider to the end. The height collapses. The shape marker barely moves — and the reader still gets the answer right.

That is the entire scientific bet, and you can test it yourself in ten seconds. Absolute readings drift with heat, damp and age. We do not read the height. We read the shape — and shape is what survives a decade outdoors.

WHY NOW, AND NOT BEFORE

The thing that stopped it has stopped being true.

Every previous attempt at this kind of computing has needed a semiconductor plant. Ours needs an anodising bath — a century-old industrial process at roughly nine to twenty-two dollars per square metre, on aluminium already sitting on the asset.

Grown, where the metal allows it

On aluminium, anodising converts the metal's own surface into oxide. Already anodised in enormous quantity: building envelopes, aircraft skin, ship superstructure, industrial plant, and the objects people carry.

Grown on a layer we put there first

Where the material is not aluminium — glass, polymer, steel — we do not switch chemistry and hope. We put a thin aluminium layer down first, then anodise that. Sprayed aluminium is already applied to offshore steel as a corrosion coating the owner pays for anyway.

That second route carries an advantage we did not design for. Sprayed aluminium is sacrificial — meant to corrode so the steel does not. So what the coating reports is the wearing away of the protection itself: how much is left. A specific, valuable question with no good answer today.

What we are not claiming, and will not until it is measured. Medical or implant applications. Space qualification. Any figure for service life. That the coating stores or generates energy — it does not; the reader brings its own power. And that a damaged coating repairs itself: bare aluminium re-oxidises in air within seconds, which is why it survives outdoors for decades, but a thick anodised layer once broken is repaired by re-coating. We would rather rule these out ourselves than have them found.
THE NUMBERS

A direction is worth its nearest checkable claim.

Corrosion costs the world an estimated $2.5 trillion a year, and $375–875 billion of that is recoverable using practice that already exists. The barrier has never been knowledge. Yet the entire market for measuring corrosion is about $0.4 billion.

The world spends roughly a hundred times more preventing corrosion than measuring it. We are not short of knowledge. We are short of sight.

THE FLOOR

Even at the worst outcome.

Professional investors price risk before they open a document, so here it is, set out exactly.

Even if the computing never works — we still have a coating read two ways: the condition of the metal underneath, and whether the part is genuine, on the surface of the thing itself.
Even if only one of those works — we still have a way to find rust underneath pipe insulation: one of the most expensive problems in industrial maintenance, needing no computing claim at all.
Even if it works only in a narrow range of conditions — we still have everything indoors or under cover: data centre cooling and power plant, electrical cabinets, aircraft interiors, sealed tanks. A narrow range does not remove the market. It tells us which one to take.
Even if nothing holds its state at all — we still have a coating that fades one way only: a record of how hard something has been worked that nobody can wind back. No power, no computing. The wearing out becomes the product.
Even at the very bottom — we still hold an answer nobody in the world has today, the money never spent on the later experiments, and the reader itself, which was never tied to this coating.

WHY WE THINK THIS WORKS

Ten of thirteen layers are already proven.

Most by other people, in journals anyone can check or products anyone can buy. Hydrogen carrying computing signals. Hydrogen moving through very thin aluminium oxide. Silicon chips reading hydrogen at thirteen million sensors a chip, sold for over a decade. Messy materials doing useful work once the reader is trained. Anodised aluminium making colour from its own structure.

Not one of the three remaining questions asks for a new discovery. Each asks whether established physics behaves the same way in a cheaper material. That is the ordinary business of research, not a leap into the unknown.

The material underneath is a century-old bath process costing a few dollars a square metre, on the most abundant metal in the earth's crust, already sitting on the buildings, aircraft, ships and plant we want to watch. No new factory, no new supply chain, no new element.

And the experiment that matters most has never been run by anyone — not run and failed, never attempted. That answer does not exist in the world today. It takes eighteen months and a defined sum to obtain, and whoever funds it holds it first.
